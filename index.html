Woken gently.
Then surely.

On your iPhone the alarm is a sequence, not a noise. A voice you chose greets you and blesses the day, quietly. Then it asks you to rise. Then music. Only if you are still asleep comes the sound that research shows wakes people best. And it ends only when the wake mission is done.

Waking is hard for everyone for the first minutes, often up to half an hour and sometimes longer1,2. No alarm removes that. This one tries not to make it worse, and never to fail.

Full alarm after 3:35, if you are still asleep.

  1. 1

    Voice, softly

    0:00 to 0:15

    “Good morning. God has given you a new day.” Then a verse, spoken as a blessing: 2 Thessalonians 3:16, word for word from the World English Bible.

    It begins quietly. In a small study, being woken brought a sudden jump in heart rate and blood pressure that did not happen when people woke on their own3; in another, forced waking left people slower and less comfortable4.

    FaithOur choice
  2. 2

    Voice, louder

    0:15 to 0:25

    “It's time to rise now. The day is waiting for you.” Twice, louder each time.

    We found no study comparing a rising alarm with a sudden one. Starting soft and rising evenly is our choice, and we say so.

    Our choice
  3. 3

    A quiet pause

    0:25 to 0:55

    30 seconds of silence.

    A chance to come up on your own. In one study, a silence between alarm bursts showed a trend in its favour, not a proof5.

    Our choice
  4. 4

    Wake up, once more

    0:55 to 1:05

    “Wake up! It's morning.”

    In smoke-alarm trials, a recorded voice woke far more sleeping children than the usual high beep: 96% against 58% in one6. Adding the child’s name did not help7, so Morrow does not say yours.

    Evidence
  5. 5

    Music rising

    1:05 to 3:35

    An original, melodic tune, rising evenly to full volume.

    People who woke to sounds they found melodic reported less grogginess8. The studies are small, and a review found the evidence not yet firm9.

    Suggestive
  6. 6

    The alarm

    from 3:35

    Low tones, 523 and 440 Hz, in the smoke-alarm rhythm, with “Wake up now! It's time.” in the gaps. Until the mission is done.

    A low square-wave tone near 520 Hz wakes people from deep sleep at lower volumes than the usual high-pitched beep5; at 75 dBA it woke 92% of hard-of-hearing adults, against 56%10. Voice and low tone together worked best for children aged nine and over11. These were emergency studies; using the signal for a morning alarm is our reasoning.

    EvidenceOur choice

Wake missions

Stop only snoozes. The alarm ends when you finish its mission: one easy question, a simple sum or a verse from your own Bible, then a photo of an everyday thing, checked on the phone. We found no study of missions; making you stand up is our choice.

Our choice

Snooze

Always there. Studies disagree on whether snoozing helps or harms12,13. After a snooze, Morrow skips the greeting and starts at “Wake up”.

Suggestive

Light

The screen warms as it rings. Dawn lamps made people feel less groggy in several studies, with mixed results on performance14,15. A phone screen is not a dawn lamp, and we do not pretend it is.

SuggestiveOur choice

Sleep cycles

Morrow does not guess your sleep stage. Phones are poor at it16,17, and waking in light sleep has not been shown to help.

Evidence

Prayer and Scripture

We found no study of prayer or Scripture at waking, so we make no health claim for them. Research links faith with better sleep, but only as association18. The verse is there because Morrow is made for people who want to begin the day with God.

Faith

What helps most

Seven hours or more, at regular times19,20. A good alarm gets you up; a good night makes it easier.

Evidence

The full sequence plays on the iPhone while Morrow is open or Sleep mode is running. With Morrow closed, iOS rings the alarm sound itself. The desktop app never rings. With headphones connected, the sequence stays under 60% of the volume, as the WHO advises21. Nothing here is medical advice.
